Chapter 01  ·  The Decade of Soul

The Seventies

Billboard Hot 100 · 1970 – 1979

Enter Soul FM →

Decade Highlights

Stevie Wonder's Classic Period — Between 1972 and 1976, Wonder released five consecutive masterworks: Music of My Mind, Talking Book, Innervisions, Fulfillingness' First Finale, and Songs in the Key of Life. Each won Album of the Year at the Grammys. Back to back to back to back to back.

Le Freak by Chic (1978) became Atlantic Records' best-selling single of all time — born the night Nile Rodgers and Bernard Edwards were turned away at Studio 54's velvet rope. Rejection never sounded so good.

Elton John placed 16 songs in the US Top 40 in 1975 alone — a single-year record that still stands. Seven consecutive #1 albums between 1972 and 1975. One extraordinary decade of excess.

Disco's spectacular collapse: In 1979, six of the year's top 10 songs were disco. Then came Disco Demolition Night — July 12, Comiskey Park. A Chicago DJ destroyed 10,000 disco records at home plate. A riot followed. An era ended in one evening.

The 70s gave us Funk, Soul, Disco, Classic Rock, and Punk — all simultaneously. No decade before or since contained that much musical revolution in one ten-year span.

Defining Trend
The Rise
of Disco

What started as underground club culture in New York became the sound of a generation. Donna Summer, Gloria Gaynor, Chic, and the Bee Gees turned the dancefloor into the most important stage in music — until the backlash burned it down in a single night at Comiskey Park.

Defining Trend
When MTV
Changed Music

August 1, 1981. MTV launched with "Video Killed the Radio Star." Within three years, how you looked was as important as how you sounded. Michael Jackson's "Thriller" didn't just sell records — it turned music into cinema.

Decade Highlights

Thriller (1982) — Michael Jackson's masterpiece spent 37 weeks at #1, sold over 66 million copies worldwide, and produced seven top-10 singles from a single album. It remains the best-selling album in history. Some records exist to never be broken.

Prince's "When Doves Cry" (1984) reached #1 with no bass line — an intentional, radical choice that made it the sound of the year. It was the best-selling US single of 1984. Prince played every instrument himself.

"Don't You (Forget About Me)" was offered to — and rejected by — Bryan Ferry, David Bowie, and Billy Idol before Simple Minds recorded it reluctantly in two hours. It became their only US #1. The lesson: always say yes.

Whitney Houston's debut (1985) produced three consecutive #1 singles — the first debut album in history to achieve that. She went on to become the most awarded female artist of all time, with 415 career accolades.

The 80s proved that image was everything — and then gave us artists whose talent was so overwhelming, the image became secondary anyway.

The Nineties

Decade Highlights

"One Sweet Day" (1995) — Mariah Carey and Boyz II Men held #1 for 16 consecutive weeks, a record that stood untouched for 23 years. When it finally fell in 2019, it fell to Lil Nas X's "Old Town Road." A fitting heir.

Nirvana's Nevermind (1992) knocked Michael Jackson's "Dangerous" off the #1 album spot. Rock's generation gap closed in a single week. The charts would never look quite the same again.

TLC's CrazySexyCool (1994) became the best-selling R&B album by a group in history — over 23 million copies worldwide. "Waterfalls" was the best-selling US single of 1995. The group sold 65 million records total.

The Spice Girls sold 85 million records in just six years, becoming the best-selling female group of all time. "Wannabe" reached #1 in 37 countries. They did it in platform shoes. In 1996. It still holds up.

The 90s were the last decade before the internet rewired everything. Every record sold was earned — in a store, in cash, with intention. The charts meant something different then.

Defining Trend
Grunge Meets
R&B

Nirvana and Mariah Carey both at #1 in the same year. Hip-hop going platinum. Country going stadium. The Billboard chart had never — and has never since — reflected such genuine diversity of sound across a single decade.

A Different Kind of Fame

One Hit
Wonders

1974 · 70s
Kung Fu Fighting
Carl Douglas

Recorded as a B-side filler in 10 minutes at the end of a studio session. Sold 11 million copies worldwide. Spent three weeks at #1. Douglas never had another charting single. Those 10 minutes paid better than most entire careers.

1979 · 70s
Escape (The Piña Colada Song)
Rupert Holmes

The last #1 single of the 1970s. A song about a man placing a personal ad to cheat on his girlfriend — who turns out to be his own girlfriend. Holmes called it "the only truly happy adultery song ever written." He had a point.

1982 · 80s
Come On Eileen
Dexys Midnight Runners

#1 on both sides of the Atlantic simultaneously. Lead singer Kevin Rowland required the entire band to wear denim dungarees over bare skin for every performance, without exception. The band split two years later, never to chart again.

1983 · 80s
99 Luftballons
Nena

A German anti-war anthem about 99 toy balloons triggering a nuclear war. #1 in 12 countries — in German. The English version was released and flopped. The original is still a fixture at every 80s night worldwide, four decades later.

1992 · 90s
I'm Too Sexy
Right Said Fred

Recorded in a single day by two bald brothers from London on a shoestring budget. Reached #1 in 16 countries. "I'm too sexy for my shirt" entered the permanent cultural lexicon. A sample appeared in Taylor Swift's "Look What You Made Me Do" — 25 years later.

1996 · 90s
Macarena
Los Del Rio

14 consecutive weeks at #1 — tied for the longest-running #1 of the decade. The two members of Los Del Rio were in their 40s when it was recorded. Their original contract meant they received a fraction of the royalties from the song that defined a summer.
